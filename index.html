Presentation 01

Julian Pullicino

B.Eng. (Hons) in Mechanical Engineering · Faculty of Engineering

Experimental and computational assessment of a parachute-based aerodynamic decelerator for atmospheric descent

Read abstract

The aerodynamic characterisation of parachute-based deceleration systems represents a critical area of research in the development of atmospheric re-entry vehicles, where reliable prediction of drag behaviour is essential to mission success. This study combined computational and experimental methods to assess the aerodynamic behaviour of a sub-scale canopy representative of the geometry of a parachute-based aerodynamic decelerator for atmospheric re-entry. Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations were conducted using ANSYS Fluent 2025 R2 to characterise the drag behaviour of the canopy both in isolation and in the presence of an upstream spherical capsule. Wind tunnel experiments were subsequently carried out on a sub-scale rigid canopy model geometrically identical to that used in the CFD simulations and tested within the same facility represented in the CFD domain. The study additionally encompassed the design and implementation of the wind tunnel experimental setup, selection and calibration of force measurement instrumentation, a trailing distance study to inform capsule-canopy configuration selection, an assessment of wind tunnel blockage effects on the predicted drag coefficient, and an evaluation of the wake flow structures and behaviour arising from both the isolated canopy and capsule-canopy configurations.

The CFD results demonstrated that the isolated canopy drag coefficient stabilised at approximately 2 across the tested velocity range, with the elevation above the unbounded literature value of 1.42 attributed to wind tunnel blockage effects. The introduction of an upstream capsule consistently reduced the canopy drag force and drag coefficient across all velocities, owing to the momentum deficit introduced by the capsule wake. Experimental validation of the isolated canopy demonstrated good agreement with CFD predictions, with percentage differences below 3% at lower and intermediate speeds, and a maximum deviation of approximately 9% at 30 𝑚/𝑠 that remained within acceptable engineering limits and could be attributed to identifiable experimental limitations rather than deficiencies in the numerical model. The combined experimental and computational approach adopted in this study demonstrated that carefully conducted investigations of this nature can provide reliable aerodynamic characterisation of sub-scale parachute canopy models, contributing to the broader body of knowledge underpinning the design of atmospheric re-entry decelerators.

Presentation 02

Ian Caruana

B.Sc. in Artificial Intelligence · Faculty of Information and Communication Technology

Low-resource Approaches for Automatic Speech Recognition for Maltese

Read abstract

Automatic speech recognition allows computers to convert spoken language into text and is now widely used for tasks such as generating captions and interacting with digital devices. However, while these systems have become highly reliable for widely spoken languages, Maltese remains difficult to support due to a lack of data. Developing speech recognition systems typically requires large amounts of transcribed speech, which are far less available for Maltese, a language spoken by approximately 500,000 people.

My project investigated whether reliable Maltese speech recognition could instead be achieved by making more effective use of the limited data already available. Rather than training a model entirely from scratch, I fine-tuned Wav2Vec-BERT, a speech model that had already learned general representations of human speech from large amounts of audio. This allowed the model to build on existing knowledge of speech while adapting to Maltese using a comparatively small collection of transcribed recordings.

I also investigated a second problem. A speech model may produce text that sounds correct when read aloud but does not necessarily follow the spelling and structure of Maltese. To address this, I trained a language model on a large collection of Maltese text and combined it with the speech model during transcription using a technique known as shallow fusion. In this way, the system could consider not only what was being heard, but also which words and word sequences were more plausible in Maltese.

The final system achieved a word error rate of 3.85% on the Mozilla Common Voice read-speech dataset, meaning that more than 96% of words were transcribed correctly. This result surpassed previous state-of-the-art performance, while on more challenging conversational speech, the model still matched existing performance despite relying on less transcribed audio.

In the end, these results demonstrate that by building on existing speech models and combining them with knowledge of the Maltese language, reliable Maltese speech-to-text is achievable without requiring the enormous datasets available for larger languages. This provides a strong foundation for further improving Maltese speech recognition using the limited resources currently available.

Presentation 03

Bernard Pullicino

B.Eng. (Hons) in Mechanical Engineering · Faculty of Engineering

Development and Evaluation of Biodegradable Calcium Phosphate/PLA Coatings for WE43 Magnesium Alloy Bone Substitutes

Read abstract

What if a bone implant could help a fractured bone while it heals and then gradually disappear when it is no longer needed? Today, implants made from metals such as titanium and stainless steel are designed to be strong and remain in the body permanently, which can result in inferior bone healing as the stiff implant carries much of the load instead of the healing bone, and may also require a second operation to remove the implant once the bone has healed. A biodegradable implant could offer a different approach, where the implant provides support for the bone while it heals and then gradually breaks down as new bone forms and grows.

This project investigated magnesium as a material for such biodegradable implants. Magnesium is particularly promising given its similar strength and stiffness to that of natural bone, is naturally found in the body, and can gradually degrade when exposed to bodily fluids. However, magnesium has an important drawback, as it can degrade too quickly, potentially losing its strength before the bone has had enough time to heal.

To address this problem, different protective coatings were investigated to slow down the degradation of magnesium, particularly ceramic and plastic-based coatings that can also gradually break down in the body to control the lifespan of the implant. In this context, calcium phosphate coatings containing calcium and phosphorus, which are important elements found in natural bone, were studied to investigate how different coating compositions and surface characteristics affected the protection of the underlying magnesium. Three calcium phosphate coatings were investigated, based on brushite, monetite + whitlockite, and hydroxyapatite. These calcium phosphate compounds are of particular interest for bone applications due to their similarity to the mineral compounds found in natural bone.

A biodegradable plastic called polylactic acid, or PLA, was also investigated as another protective coating, which is widely used in applications such as 3D printing. The coatings were tested in a laboratory solution at approximately 37oC to reproduce the salts and conditions that an implant would experience inside the body over a six-week period.

The results showed that the calcium phosphate coatings provided the most effective protection. Of the three calcium phosphate coatings, the coating containing monetite and whitlockite showed the best overall performance. This particular coating formed a strong protective layer and greatly reduced corrosion of the magnesium substrate, producing very little hydrogen gas during the six-week period, which is a byproduct of magnesium corrosion. In comparison, although PLA initially protected the magnesium for a few days, it developed cracks and separated from the surface, allowing corrosion to occur underneath the coating.

These results highlight that the aim of a biodegradable implant is not to prevent the material from breaking down completely, but to control when it degrades. The ultimate goal is an implant that can provide support while the bone heals, gradually transferring the load to newly formed bone, and eventually disappearing once it has done its job. In this regard, the calcium phosphate coating composed of monetite and whitlockite showed the best protection and degradation control among the coatings investigated. However, further testing using a larger number of samples is required to confirm the effectiveness and reliability of the coatings.

Presentation 04

Francesca Micallef

B.Sc. (Hons) in Applied Biomedical Science · Faculty of Health Sciences

Loss of ALS risk factor SCFD1 and its effect on survival in Drosophila

Read abstract

Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) is a neurodegenerative disorder that targets motor neurons, the specialised nerve cells that control our muscles. The progressive breakdown of these cells leads to the hallmark ALS symptoms such as progressive muscle atrophy, paralysis, and eventual respiratory failure, ultimately significantly shortening the patient’s lifespan. Although in most patients the exact cause of ALS remains unknown, studies have uncovered several risk and causative genes linked to this disease, including SCFD1, a gene factor identified amongst the Maltese ALS patient population. However, to date, there have been no studies dedicated to uncovering the effect of this mutated gene on patient lifespan. This project sought to investigate this gap using Drosophila melanogaster, known as the common fruit fly, as a model organism to investigate the effect of the functional loss of SCFD1, which mimics the mutated gene found in ALS patients. The flies were genetically altered to produce offspring with targeted SCFD1 knockdown (reduced levels of gene expression) in various tissues, namely in brain neurons and glia which form part of the central nervous system. Findings from testing adult male flies revealed impaired motor function, so diminished flying and climbing abilities, as well as a shortened adult lifespan. In sum, this study confirmed the link between reduced levels of SCFD1 and a general decline in Drosophila motor function and survival, both symptoms mimicking core clinical ALS features observed in human patients.

Presentation 05

Matthew Fiott

B.Sc. in ICT · Faculty of Information and Communication Technology

VHDL Implementation to Compute the DCT-II

Read abstract

High-resolution video contains an enormous amount of data, making the transmission of uncompressed video highly impractical. Video compression addresses this problem by representing the same or very similar information using significantly less data.

While compression algorithms can be executed on general-purpose CPUs, their largely sequential nature can become a bottleneck when processing the large amounts of data as seen in images and especially videos. Parallel processing, as used by GPUs, provides a more efficient approach by performing many operations simultaneously.

One mathematical operation commonly used in such compression is the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT), whose calculations are well suited to parallel processing. This project explored the design and VHDL implementation of a dedicated hardware unit capable of computing the DCT at high frequency. Although the unit does not perform compression independently, it was designed as a potential component within a larger compression system.

The project demonstrates how highly specialised hardware can accelerate computationally intensive applications, while highlighting the relationship between the theoretical side of mathematics and the practical side of computer engineering to give us solutions for real-world problems.

Presentation 06

Ryan Paul Camilleri

Master of Science (by Research) · Faculty of Information and Communication Technology

Secondary Emission Monitor Grids for a Muon Collider Proton Linear Accelerator: A Feasibility Study

Read abstract

Particle accelerators are often described as discovery machines, allowing us to explore the fundamental building blocks of matter. Behind every successful accelerator, however, is a network of instruments that acts as its eyes and ears, continuously monitoring the particle beam, diagnosing problems, and helping optimise its performance. But how do you measure a particle beam that is powerful enough to damage the instrument used to measure it?

This question is particularly relevant for the proton driver of a proposed future muon collider, where enormous numbers of protons (up to 5 × 1014 particles) are expected to be delivered in very short, intense bursts. Such conditions pose a serious challenge for secondary emission monitor (SEM) grids, which comprise an array of thin wires to measure the properties of particle beams. When intercepting an intense proton beam, these wires absorb energy and can heat rapidly, potentially deforming, melting, or breaking.

This study investigated whether SEM grids could withstand the extreme conditions expected in a future muon collider proton driver. Different materials, including tungsten, tantalum, molybdenum, titanium, silicon carbide, and carbon fibre, and a range of wire diameters were examined using particle-transport simulations and three-dimensional thermal and mechanical modelling. The models were validated against experimental observations from CERN, including instances of wire heating realised through measurements of thermionic emission and a documented instrument- damage event, before being used to explore the operational limits of SEM grids under the projected beam conditions.

The results revealed a significant difference between conventional metallic wires and fibre-based materials. While metallic wires were found to have relatively narrow operating windows and could experience melting, mechanical yielding, or fracture, carbon-fibre-based wires demonstrated substantially greater resilience, remaining within safe operating conditions across a much broader range of beam parameters.

Beyond identifying promising materials for future beam diagnostics, the study demonstrates how simulations and experiments can be combined to predict when scientific instruments themselves become vulnerable to the phenomena they are designed to measure. These findings provide a basis for developing more robust beam diagnostics for the next generation of high-intensity particle accelerators.

Presentation 07

Maria Bajada

M.Sc. in Midwifery · Faculty of Health Sciences

Lesbian Couples’ Experiences of Maternity Care

Read abstract

Purpose: Internationally, greater visibility of diverse family structures has highlighted how heteronormative assumptions still shape maternity care. Research shows that lesbian couples often face inconsistent recognition, exclusionary language, and variable inclusion within perinatal services. In Malta, despite advances in LGBTQ+ rights, no studies have explored their maternity care experiences. This study addresses this gap by examining how lesbian couples in Malta navigate pregnancy, childbirth, and early postnatal care.

Aim and Objectives: The study aimed to explore the maternity care experiences of lesbian couples in Malta. The objectives were to explore their experiences during the antenatal, intrapartum and early postpartum period; to enquire into the feelings evoked while receiving care; to understand their specific needs and preferences; and to examine how they constructed meaning around these experiences.

Methodology: A qualitative design informed by Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) was used. Audio-recorded semi-structured interviews were conducted with five lesbian couples who were at least 18 years old, first-time parents, and had given birth in Malta within the past three years. I had no professional involvement in their care. Interviews were transcribed verbatim and analysed using IPA principles, with Queer Theory guiding interpretation.

Results: Navigating Maternity Care, Recognition as a Legitimate Family, and Language and Belonging, were the three overarching themes which emerged from the participants’ experiences. These themes captured the affirming interpersonal encounters and persistent structural barriers that participants’ experiences whilst they sought visibility, legitimacy and emotional safety as they navigated through maternity care.

Conclusions: While many couples experienced inclusive care, heteronormative structures, especially documentation and language, remain embedded in maternity services. This study provides the first empirical insight into lesbian couples’ maternity care experiences in Malta and highlights the need for inclusive policies, institutional reform and staff training.
